# Adaptive policy audit

## Source and evidence

The pre-change scheduler was audited at `a4d8df8efd6e1a99ed58e331f54ddcd16741c729`. The four Protocol V2 manifests, raw run JSON, traces, aggregates, plots, and analysis tests were inspected before edits. `scripts/verify_experiment.py` reported zero failures and checked 128, 88, 32, and 50 local PPM pixel hashes respectively. Independent raw-record medians reproduce the handoff values. These old results are observations, never a tuning sample that can also prove a revised policy.

## Exact pre-change rule

Fixed dynamic sends one 16×16 microtile per assignment. Adaptive starts each worker at two tiles. After a worker returns an entire batch, its observed rate is `pixels / sum(tile render seconds including injected stall)`. The estimate is set to the first observation, then updated as `0.5 × previous + 0.5 × observation`. The next batch is `min(remaining, floor(clamp(rate × 0.050 / 256, 1, 8)), max(1, floor(remaining / (2 × worker_count))), 8)`. Edge tiles still use 256 as the prediction divisor even when they contain fewer pixels. The coordinator owns a contiguous next-tile cursor and each worker has at most one outstanding assignment. It blocks waiting for a returned batch before updating the estimate; work inside a pending batch is invisible to the policy. A `0.050` second target is per predicted batch, not a measured service-level guarantee. Integer truncation creates step changes; the fairness cap tapers only from *undispatched* tiles, so already assigned long batches remain in flight. Worker speed directly changes the predicted size after a completed batch, but stale measurements can persist through a long batch.

## What the V2 data show

At 256×144, tile 16, each render has 144 microtiles. In the seven measured natural-scene blocks, fixed dynamic dispatched 144 assignments every time. Adaptive median counts were 42 (imbalanced), 36 (reflective), 50 (scheduler_stress), and 30 (uniform). That is why adaptive sends fewer batches: the rate estimate often hits multi-tile bins, up to the cap of eight. These are real control changes, not a renamed fixed policy.

Fewer dispatches did not consistently reduce wall time. On the small V2 workload, adaptive/fixed medians in seconds were 1.089/1.085 (imbalanced), 0.921/0.943 (reflective), 1.296/1.259 (scheduler_stress), and 0.619/0.609 (uniform). The synthetic two-times-slower-rank experiment was 1.739/1.671. The scheduler_stress adaptive trace has an early eight-tile assignment (tiles 68–75, worker 1) that completed at about 0.463 s after dispatch at 0.190 s, while worker 2's simultaneous eight-tile assignment (76–83) completed near 0.348 s. This illustrates within-batch heterogeneity and delayed feedback; it does not by itself establish the net cause of the runtime difference. Old rank-0 `result_wait_seconds` is mostly blocking while workers render. It is not pure communication or scheduling overhead.

The new diagnostic counters define the **90% completion tail** as `render_wall_seconds − time when the coordinator has first received whole batches containing at least 90% of microtiles`. Because completion is observed per returned batch, this is a batch-granularity boundary, not each tile's actual finish instant. `last_worker_spread_seconds` is the latest minus earliest *final batch receipt time* among rendering workers. Both include coordinator receipt timing and must be read alongside worker work and MPI-call occupancy. Per-worker effective rate is pixels divided by render plus injected stall seconds. Coordinator handling duration includes result payload receipt, placement, throughput update, and next dispatch; it is not CPU-only coordinator time. MPI send/receive timings are blocking-call occupancy, not pure wire time.

Normal benchmark mode omits the in-memory assignment trace and still records scalar scheduler counters. Diagnostic trace mode records every assignment and its batch size, dispatch/completion times, remaining tiles on completion, and rate estimate at dispatch. CSV writing is outside the render timer in both modes. Trace overhead is measured separately under `results/diagnostic/adaptive-v3/`.

The JSON field `worker_requests` counts worker calls that wait for an assignment, including the final termination receive. The protocol is coordinator-push: workers send no separate request packet. Treat the field as receive-loop iterations, not network request messages.

## Separate medium diagnostics

All four studies below are explicitly exploratory, have only three measured blocks per condition, and are stored outside Protocol V2 formal results. Their binary/source hashes and exact commands are in their manifests and raw records. Do not treat differences across separate studies as paired effects.

| `20260928T074937Z-f3ae58ea` | Trace-on versus normal | Fixed normal/trace medians 3.295/3.210 s; adaptive normal/trace 3.240/3.210 s. Individual paired blocks changed direction. No consistent material trace penalty was established by this small, noisy test. |

The fixed-size study exposes the main tradeoff. On imbalanced medium, changing batch 1 to batch 8 cut assignments from 144 to 18, and median worker assignment-wait sum from 0.012 to 0.002 s, while median wall time grew from 3.177 to 3.501 s and last-worker spread from 0.013 to 0.461 s. On scheduler_stress, the same change cut assignment-wait sum from 0.011 to 0.002 s, but wall time grew from 3.775 to 4.236 s and spread from 0.018 to 0.548 s. MPI send/receive call occupancy was about 0.002 s at batch 1 on both scenes. Coordinator handling was about 0.001 s or less. These measured costs are too small for traffic reduction alone to compensate for a several-tenths-of-a-second late batch. `last_worker_spread_seconds` includes coordinator receipt timing, so it is a descriptive proxy rather than direct worker idle-time accounting.

In the later frozen **formal** medium experiment `20260928T075743Z-cf62886e`, adaptive median assignment counts were 92/89/95/81 on imbalanced/reflective/scheduler_stress/uniform, versus 144 for fixed dynamic. Coordinator handling medians were about 0.0006–0.0008 s adaptive and 0.0009–0.0010 s fixed; MPI send/receive-call sums were about 0.0011–0.0015 s adaptive and 0.0016–0.0019 s fixed. Worker assignment-wait sums differed by only about 0.003–0.004 s. The 90%-completion tails were nearly the same within each scene, and both policies' compute imbalance was near zero. The measured traffic savings are real but small compared with render duration and run-to-run changes. The three irregular-scene paired adaptive/fixed intervals included zero; uniform showed a roughly 0.030 s adaptive penalty. This experiment does not isolate a single precise cause for every small policy gap. It does reject the simple claim that fewer assignments or matched accumulated work *must* lower wall time.

The medium imbalanced adaptive diagnostic trace at `20260928T073928Z-ca32eece/raw/imbalanced-adaptive-50-b0.trace.csv` shows the fairness cap tapering to single-tile assignments near completion: the final eight received assignments had `remaining_on_completion` 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, and 0. This directly shows that the default heuristic avoids a coarse final *undispatched* queue, though it cannot retract an earlier large batch already in flight.

At coarse batch sizes, one receipt can jump the completed count from below 90% straight to 100%, making the batch-boundary `tail90_seconds` nearly zero. That metric is therefore not comparable across coarse batch sizes without the last-worker spread and trace. The latter gives the clearer warning here. The results support a workload granularity explanation for fixed size 1's strong performance and show that adaptive saves very little coordinator/worker-wait time on this single host. They do **not** establish a universal ranking of batch policies. The default adaptive policy was retained because neither the target-duration nor initial-size sensitivity study yielded a specific, robust correction.
